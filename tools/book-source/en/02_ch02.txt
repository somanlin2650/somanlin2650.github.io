#H1 2 — Preservation, Composition, and Return
Hans's hoofbeats were counted as an answer; the answer was read as evidence of arithmetic ability. After Pfungst changed the tests, the same hoofbeats received another explanation. The turning point in the previous chapter lay between these steps.

For the moment, we can lay out the researcher's work: obtain material, keep records, make comparisons, form a judgement, and arrange another test. As new results arrive, the original explanation may change. Sometimes what is tested, and how it is recorded, must change too.

**Material → Representation → Operation → Judgement and action → Results and new material.**

This sequence offers an initial map. Material consists of the experience and records available on this occasion. Representation preserves some of their differences; operations let us compare, infer, rearrange, or try things out. Judgement can guide action, which changes the environment and brings new material. The knower's abilities, the environment, and available resources support the whole process; purpose gives particular differences and results their importance.

The arrows do not mean that everyone settles on a purpose first and then thinks in order. We may begin doing something before discovering what we want to ask; a skilled body need not first translate what it perceives into sentences. The map helps locate dependencies, without requiring every act of understanding to follow one procedure. Some results arrive much later; others can no longer be checked. These missing routes of return also need to remain visible. Purpose and judgement appear in the map because we are examining how understanding works. This does not give the stones, rivers, or diseases being understood purposes or cognition of their own.

#H2 Can a Difference Survive the Next Step?
Preservation, composition, and return are the three relations this book uses to examine transformations. Returning to Hans makes their different questions clearer.

**Preservation** asks what remains distinguishable after a change of form. If the record retains only the number of correct answers, whether the questioner knew the answer disappears from the data. To distinguish arithmetic from responses to bodily signals, that difference must survive. It may matter less for another purpose; what should be preserved always needs to be explained in relation to use.

**Composition** asks whether what one step supplies is enough to support the next. The right number of taps supports the claim that the horse performed correctly in these circumstances. Reaching the claim that he understood arithmetic requires further work to rule out other explanations. Each step may look plausible on its own, while the combined inference outruns the evidence at the point of connection.

**Return** asks whether later discoveries have a route back to change earlier methods. A failed test can be recorded as a fluke, or it can prompt a review of categories and a new experimental design. When Pfungst brought the questioner's knowledge and the horse's line of sight into the comparison, he changed more than the answer to a single problem.

Each relation has its own work to do. Complete records do not guarantee sound connections between inferences; recognising a gap does not guarantee that anyone can change the test. Conversely, even a willingness to repeat the work cannot recover every difference once the original records are gone. Return means a route for investigation and revision, not a promise that time or information can flow backwards.

#H2 What Changes at the First and Second Orders?
Testing one more problem by the existing method may change our judgement of that performance. Including the questioner's knowledge in the test changes how the judgement is formed. **First-order understanding uses existing methods to address things; second-order understanding also makes the methods of forming, connecting, checking, and revising judgements open to change.**

The distinction is not one of profundity. Thinking through the same answer again may leave the method untouched; changing a field in a record may enable the entire test to distinguish something previously invisible. Second-order understanding must make a difference of this kind.

Nor are the rules for changing methods exempt. Is an anomaly enough to justify repeating a study? Who can raise it, and who decides whether it deserves attention? Arrangements that repeatedly miss important problems also need reconsideration. We need not ascend through endless levels before every action. A concrete difficulty can guide us to the level that needs another look.

#H2 How One Transformation Hands Over to Another
Imagine a water-supply investigation; for now, consider only how its data connect. Household addresses are mapped, drinking-water sources are classified, and deaths are compared across categories. The addresses must correspond to the period under investigation, the categories to the sources being studied, and the death counts to appropriate denominators. If any connection goes wrong, precise calculations may answer a different question.

A dataset counting people cannot be compared directly with one counting households supplied merely because both columns say 'mortality rate'. Nor may an unanswered interview quietly become a negative response when tables are merged. Such small handovers determine whether individually useful tasks can combine into a greater ability. Snow's investigation in Chapter 6 will make this concrete.

Comparing groups separately before assembling an overview may preserve very different possibilities from compressing everything into an average and only then asking about group differences. Identical names do not guarantee identical contents; a change of order may also lose relations we need.

The unity sought here rests on these traceable relations. Mathematical proof, historical inquiry, and bodily practice each involve indispensable work. We want to study how they develop abilities, how they can connect under suitable conditions, and how new results can change established methods. Calling them all 'information' is far from enough.

#H2 Where to Begin Checking
Six questions can help locate the work needed for a particular judgement. They indicate places to examine and may be omitted, divided, or rephrased as circumstances require.

Begin with purpose: **what do we want to understand or accomplish?** Predict an outcome, explain a cause, complete an action, understand someone, or reconsider what is worth pursuing? Clarifying the use helps establish what would count as a gain.

Locate ability and circumstance: **who is understanding, and who bears the consequences?** Knowers bring existing abilities, positions, and limits. Those affected may have material absent from the framework, yet may never have had a chance to help define the problem.

Follow the material: **which differences does the representation preserve?** Tables, maps, statements, sensations, and models retain particular relations. Establish where the material came from and how it was selected, then examine what the representation keeps. Once a needed distinction has been merged away, the representation alone cannot restore it.

With a representation in place: **which operations are available?** Some representations suit comparison, others proof; some support rapid action, others the reconstruction of someone's reasons. A framework's contents must be considered alongside the work they enable.

Examine what supports that work: **on which conditions does the ability depend?** Sources, environmental regularities, time, skills, tools, and cooperation may all contribute to success. When conditions change, past performance must be checked against the new use.

Finally, trace the destination of revision: **when results return, which level can change?** Sometimes a number needs correction; sometimes a representation, skill, or procedure needs changing. New experience can also undermine the original purpose. If a framework permits revised answers but forbids reconsidering questions, what it never asked will struggle to enter learning.

The questions locate work. A simple problem may need two; a new field may require finer divisions. Transfer succeeds when concrete work is identified. Repeating shared names is insufficient.

These six questions do not occupy the same level. Representations and operations are components of a process; knowers, environments, and resources support their operation. Purpose shapes selection and evaluation; revision can act on any of these. Restoring the questions to their relations within transformations helps us see what else may need to change when an answer changes.

This map gives the reading ahead a direction. Part Two examines what transformations preserve. Part Three asks who and what sustain the ability to understand. Part Four brings judgement into contact with purpose and cost. Part Five asks how results can change the conditions that produce understanding. Each part unfolds the same relations while putting this initial map to further tests.

We begin with a transit map. The city remains the same; the lines on paper change, and with them what people can do.
