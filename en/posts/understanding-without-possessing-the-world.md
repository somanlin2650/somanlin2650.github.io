When a system answers a question correctly, has it understood? If it can explain, predict, and propose effective approaches in unfamiliar situations, what more must it do before we acknowledge a form of knowledge?

AI makes these questions difficult to leave at the level of abstraction. A system may use internal representations unlike ours and achieve results through training, search, and tools. We must decide which results deserve confidence, which merely happen to work, and which failures require more reasoning or a different way of representing the problem.

The same questions turn back on us. When we understand something, do we possess a complete internal version of it, or have we acquired capacities to distinguish, infer, act, and revise?

This essay argues for the latter. Understanding can answer to truth without requiring possession of a complete copy of the world. The more productive question is: **How can a finite knower, situated within the world, form reliable and revisable representations?**

This does not replace truth with convenience or announce the end of philosophy. When mechanisms of inquiry can be constructed, philosophy gains another way to work: state the conditions behind a claim, inspect its inferences, construct counterexamples, and compare how different approaches fare against the world.

## A map need not resemble the whole world
{: #s1 }

Imagine three people entering the same city. One needs to change trains, another to identify places likely to flood, and a third to plan a walk. They will not need exactly the same map.

A transit map can straighten winding tracks, enlarge a crowded city center, and omit most streets. If it preserves station connections and transfers, these distortions may not interfere with its purpose. Give it to someone investigating flooding, however, and the omitted elevation, drainage, and surface information may be precisely what matters.

![Three conceptual maps on a desk emphasize terrain, transport, and flood risk, illustrating how purposes select different information.](/assets/img/articles/aei-task-maps.webp){: width="1659" height="948" }
_Different purposes call for different representations of an area. This is an AI-generated conceptual scene, not geographic evidence or a source for navigation or risk assessment._

Two points belong together. A map's purpose affects which distinctions deserve representation. Once that purpose is specified, however, the map cannot be drawn arbitrarily. Incorrect transfer connections cannot be excused by saying that everyone has a perspective.

Representations are selected in context, while claims made through them can still be true or false. A transit map may correctly answer a routing question without being qualified to answer a question about flood depth. A boundary of knowledge is crossed when success on one class of questions becomes a claim to mastery of the entire object.

This relationship runs through [No View Is the Whole](/en/posts/no-view-is-the-whole/). A limited perspective gives us reason to identify the choices underlying a judgment and the circumstances in which those choices cease to work.

## What does being inside the world actually limit?
{: #s2 }

Brains, notebooks, instruments, computers, and research institutions all belong to the world. Contact with it requires interfaces; storing information takes space; computation takes time. Tools extend capabilities while introducing costs and possible failures. AI research on embedded agency also examines the difficulties that arise when an agent is part of its environment rather than an observer outside it.[^embedded]

This does not immediately prove that no part of the world could contain a complete description of it.

A large system may be generated by a short rule, and describing an object does not require duplicating each of its parts. Being part of the world, by itself, does not rule out a compact and complete theory. A defensible limitation must specify the possible states, the available resources, and the questions to be answered.

At least three difficulties should be distinguished:

| Limitation | What happens | Is more reasoning enough? |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Observation | States requiring different answers look identical through the available interface | Without additional information, further reasoning cannot distinguish them |
| Representation | A summary, classification, or memory scheme removes a distinction that was available | The system needs to retrieve evidence or change what it preserves |
| Computation | The rules and necessary information are available, but obtaining an answer exceeds the budget | Sometimes; other cases require a new algorithm, approximation, or narrower question |

These limitations can coexist. A wrong answer alone generally does not identify which one caused the failure. Asking an Agent to think longer is therefore not a universal remedy. If it cannot observe an important signal, more fluent reasoning may simply extend its blind spot. This connects with the need for external observation discussed in [When AI Cannot See Its Own Hypoxia](/en/posts/when-ai-cannot-see-its-own-hypoxia/).

The immediate demand of finitude is to stop counting observations not yet obtained, distinctions not preserved, and computations not completed as parts of the understanding we already possess.

## A small example: reasoning cannot restore a lost distinction
{: #s3 }

Consider two pairs of numbers: A contains 40 and 60; B contains 0 and 100. If we retain only their means, both become 50.

For the question “What is the mean?”, this summary is sufficient. For “Is any value greater than 80?”, A requires no and B requires yes. The summary is now insufficient.

![The pairs 40 and 60, and 0 and 100, both become a mean of 50, although only the second pair contains a value above 80.](/assets/img/articles/aei-lost-distinction.webp){: width="1600" height="1000" }
_The summary has not lost everything. It has lost the particular distinction required by the new question. The identical mean cannot guarantee recovery of two different answers._

This yields a small but useful proposition. Let $$x$$ be an original state, $$r(x)$$ its retained representation, and $$q(x)$$ the desired answer. If two states satisfy

$$
r(x_1)=r(x_2),\qquad q(x_1)\ne q(x_2),
$$

then no answering procedure $$g$$ receiving only that representation can guarantee a correct answer for both states.

The reason is straightforward: identical input gives a deterministic procedure the same output. Since the correct answers differ, at least one must be wrong. Randomization allows guessing, but cannot guarantee correctness in both cases either. This is an elementary boundary on representational sufficiency, not a newly discovered deep theorem.

If there are more possible original states than available representation states, some states must be merged. Yet merging states does not imply an error on the present task. The merged states must require different answers to a relevant question. A transit map can omit many distinctions while preserving the relations needed for transfers.

Good abstraction therefore neither preserves everything indiscriminately nor discards as much as possible. It **preserves the distinctions a question requires while reducing the burden of what it does not**.

Better representation need not discard information at all. Notation, indexing, or diagrams can preserve the same relations while making them much easier to use. Representational value concerns both what is retained and which operations become accessible.

## Why knowing the rule need not mean knowing the answer
{: #s4 }

Keeping the information does not settle every difficulty.

The cellular automaton Rule 110 is a useful reminder. Each cell has two possible states. Its next state depends only on itself and its two immediate neighbors. Eight cases specify the whole local rule. Repeated updates can nevertheless produce structures whose behavior is difficult to anticipate by inspection.

![The eight local update cases of Rule 110 and a finite simulation beginning with a single active cell, with time running downward.](/assets/img/articles/aei-rule110.webp){: width="1600" height="1000" }
_The upper panel specifies the local rule; the lower panel shows 120 finite update steps. Visual complexity is neither mathematical incompressibility nor a proof of undecidability._

Three questions must be separated: whether the rule has a short description; whether a particular evolution can be computed; and whether a general procedure answers a class of questions over all admissible initial configurations and an unbounded future.

The finite example shown here can be simulated directly. Cook's research established that Rule 110 supports universal computation under suitable configurations. Certain unbounded long-term questions can consequently inherit undecidability from computation theory. This does not mean that every finite image is uncomputable or that every question lacks a shortcut.[^cook]

Nor does a complicated-looking image necessarily contain a large amount of incompressible information. A short rule, simple initial condition, and finite step count already provide a short description of the resulting image. The important distinction is that **a short description need not make the desired answer cheap to obtain**.

Understanding a generative rule, computing its consequences, and knowing its scope are related but different abilities. Even an exceptionally compact theory of the world would not automatically remove the costs of observation, initial-state specification, or computation.

## Machine learning makes the choices in knowing explicit
{: #s5 }

The philosophical relevance of machine learning does not require identifying neural networks with brains. It begins with the possibility of specifying what a model receives, what it learns, how errors are measured, and where its performance is checked.

In supervised learning, a model $$f$$ takes an input $$x$$ and predicts a target $$y$$. For a data distribution $$D$$ and loss function $$\ell$$, the population risk is

$$
R_D(f)=\mathbb E_{(x,y)\sim D}[\ell(f(x),y)].
$$

Training generally does not provide the complete distribution or direct access to this expectation. What is available is a finite sample and an empirical risk:

$$
\widehat R_S(f)=\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}\ell(f(x_i),y_i).
$$

Learning also depends on the hypothesis class, regularization, optimization method, and computational budget. Low training error is not low error on unknown data. Successful testing provides evidence, not a guarantee covering every future situation. Learning theory investigates, among other things, the conditions connecting finite samples to generalization.[^uml]

Even low average loss under $$D$$ does not imply that every input receives a nearly correct answer. Rare cases may remain poor. A change in distribution may invalidate an earlier guarantee. Even with the distribution unchanged, a different question may require features the model did not retain.

The “true target” also needs care. Some settings posit a true generating function. In others, outcomes are stochastic, and optimal prediction means an optimal decision relative to a distribution and loss. Neither requirement amounts to possessing the world itself.

The information bottleneck method addresses a related trade-off: reduce the information a representation $$Z$$ carries about input $$X$$, while retaining information relevant to target $$Y$$.[^ib] It supplies tools for studying task-relevant compression. Change the target, and discarded information may matter again. It should not be treated as a settled account of how every neural network learns.

These approaches suggest an account of knowledge in which a finite representation can support specified inferences and withstand appropriate checks. It has both achievements and boundaries. Acknowledging its boundaries does not make its achievements unreal.

## AI asks us to state our criteria for understanding
{: #s6 }

We often recognize understanding through experience: something clicks, a relation becomes visible, the next step becomes clear. Such experiences matter, but cannot simply become the sole standard for evaluating another kind of system.

AlphaGeometry offers a concrete example. It combines a neural model proposing useful auxiliary constructions with a symbolic engine carrying out deductions. Its results on a collection of olympiad geometry problems show how different mechanisms can jointly produce checkable reasoning. The result has a defined scope; it does not establish mastery of all mathematics, much less a solution to philosophy.[^ag]

Still, when a result can be checked publicly, an internal process unlike ours is insufficient reason to deny all epistemic value. Conversely, a human-sounding explanation does not establish understanding. Fluent reasons may be constructed after the fact without reflecting what actually produced the answer.

Practical criteria can be separated. Can the system maintain relevant abilities in new situations? Identify the conditions on which a conclusion depends? Revise an error using new evidence? Discover a distinction missing from a failing classification? Where intervention is possible, distinguish correlation from causation and submit to intervention-based checks?

These criteria need not collapse into one score or apply equally to every question. They make understanding a description of identifiable capabilities and gaps. Subjective experience, consciousness, and whether some kinds of understanding require feeling remain separate questions; task success does not settle them collectively.

[From Reaction to Heterogeneous Rationality](/en/posts/from-reaction-to-heterogeneous-rationality/) considers how humans and AI may follow different representational and inferential paths. A further implication follows: when intuitions are not shared, public intermediate steps, reproducible operations, and mutually checkable results become important interfaces for collaboration.

If future AI systems surpass humans at finding contradictions, constructing counterexamples, or comparing arguments in some philosophical work, that would be a gain in capability. It would not automatically confer authority to decide values or remove the obligation to provide reasons.

## Philosophy can borrow from learning without becoming only optimization
{: #s7 }

Bringing machine-learning methods into philosophy does not require turning every question into next-word prediction. What can usefully transfer is the habit of separating tasks, representations, errors, and revisions.

A discussion of fairness might begin by asking which conflict the concept addresses. Whose circumstances are represented? Which are merged by a statistic? Can two requirements both called fair be satisfied together? What costs are acceptable, and who has authority to accept them for someone else?

Data, models, and formal arguments can advance some parts of the inquiry. Others require public normative deliberation. Once an objective is encoded in a loss function, optimization can seek better performance under that specification. It cannot establish that the specification is justified.

Effective persuasion is not sufficient evidence of truth. Better managerial performance does not automatically make an arrangement reasonable for those being managed. A stable and persistently successful institution is not justified by those facts alone.

Evaluating a representation relative to questions and costs is therefore no license for relativism. It asks us to expose those questions and costs to scrutiny: Who is missing? What evidence would change the judgment? Is this goal worth pursuing? The public examination discussed in [A Morality We Can Revise](/en/posts/a-morality-we-can-revise/) remains indispensable.

## A few explicit premises, rather than a completed philosophy
{: #s8 }

This approach can be organized under the working name *Axiomatic Embedded Inquiry*, or AEI: inquiry by finite knowers situated within the world. The name identifies a direction of work—making premises and inferences explicit enough for others to locate a counterexample. It is a revisable formal outline, not a completed universal axiom system.

First specify the kind of knower under discussion. It obtains evidence through interfaces and has finite resources for storing and operating on representations. Empirical claims about the external world cannot become valid merely by its declaration. Formal claims may instead be checked by proofs under explicitly stated axioms and inference rules. Different claims need corresponding methods of verification.

A context of inquiry can provisionally be written as

$$
C=(D,Q,\ell,B,V).
$$

Here $$D$$ is a distribution over relevant situations; $$Q$$ a set of questions; $$\ell$$ a measure of error or decision cost; $$B$$ a resource budget; and $$V$$ a verification procedure. Where a distribution cannot reasonably be specified, a set of scenarios and worst-case analysis may be more suitable. Values may enter the choice of questions and costs, but their authorship and contestability must remain explicit. A symbol cannot discharge that responsibility.

Only after specifying these conditions can we ask precisely whether a representation is sufficient. For each $$q\in Q$$, does a procedure $$g_q$$ exist that can obtain the required answer from $$r(x)$$ within the budget? Approximate requirements must additionally state their tolerances and domain.

The elementary proposition above supplies one necessary condition. If a representation merges states that a relevant question must distinguish, guaranteed correctness is impossible without additional information. Conversely, retaining every necessary distinction still leaves the task of finding an executable answering procedure. Informational sufficiency does not guarantee affordable computation.

The framework organizes connected work: preserve necessary distinctions, combine usable relations, and submit conclusions to appropriate checks. Upon failure, return to evidence gathering, representation building, or question selection. This connects with the site's recurring themes of retention, combination, and return, while making the conditions for comparison explicit.

![An inquiry loop obtains evidence from an external situation, builds a representation, produces a claim, and checks it. Failure can lead back to observation or representation; changes to goals and costs require separate deliberation.](/assets/img/articles/aei-inquiry-loop.webp){: width="1600" height="1100" }
_A return must reach an actionable modification. Rewording is not new evidence; changing a representation does not authorize secretly changing the scoring standard._

## Revisability must be something a system can actually do
{: #s9 }

There is a considerable distance between welcoming criticism and allowing criticism to change a system.

An Agent may perform routine tasks well without retaining failure records. It may record errors but lack access to original evidence, or have no authority to change a summary procedure responsible for repeated failures. Promising improvement after every mistake does not supply the missing capability.

One testable design separates several functions. A routine executor handles established tasks. A failure record preserves inputs, outputs, grounds, and uncertainty. A revision process proposes additional observations or a changed representation. An independent validation process tests the proposal on cases not repeatedly used to tune it. Versioning and rollback allow unsuccessful changes to be withdrawn.

This is a candidate architecture, not a guarantee of improvement. Recording and validation consume resources. A mistaken revision may damage established capabilities. Its value must be evaluated together with its costs.

Two kinds of change particularly need separating. Adding elevation data after discovering that a model misses flood depth improves its treatment of an existing question. Discovering that a damage-reduction objective sacrifices some people's housing rights calls for renewed deliberation about acceptable arrangements. That requires affected people's participation and authorization; a model cannot resolve it by silently lowering the weight assigned to their losses.

Actual revisability requires identifiable errors, preservable evidence, accessible points of modification, and procedures for checking the consequences. It is a cognitive capacity supported by tools and institutions.

## How could these claims be tested?
{: #s10 }

Begin with a minimal setting. Two equally likely states require answers 0 and 1, respectively. The observation is identical in both, and no other information is correlated with the answer. However long the system reasons, its average accuracy using only this observation cannot exceed one half. That ceiling follows from the setup; it is not an empirical discovery awaiting a benchmark.

Allow one reliable observational bit distinguishing the states, and the informational conditions change. Alternatively, let the system initially observe the distinction but lose it in a summary. This tests a change in retention. The two experiments concern observation and representation, respectively, and should not be conflated.

More realistic comparisons could give different Agents the same total budgets for computation, storage, and queries. They would first handle one task class, then face questions or situations reserved in advance. Strategies could include longer reasoning, updating existing parameters, acquiring observations, and revising representations. External queries and revision costs must count against the budgets too.

Evaluation should extend beyond final accuracy: time to detect failure, additional evidence required, regression on old capabilities, transfer to cases held out from revision, and false alarms that mistake random fluctuation for a need to rebuild.

If the advantage of representational revision disappears under fair resource budgets, arises only from privileged access to more data, or fails on reserved cases, those results constrain the original claim. The appropriate response is to narrow its conditions or abandon the design.

Such studies cannot resolve epistemology all at once. They can expose some claims to the risk of failure. Philosophy helps establish what was compared, how much success demonstrates, and which conclusions still exceed the evidence.

## Truth remains; completeness requires more than success
{: #s11 }

This approach has predecessors. Peirce situated inquiry within the disturbance, examination, and revision of belief. Carnap's work on explication sought more precise concepts while considering their theoretical usefulness.[^peirce][^carnap] Learning theory, information theory, and research on bounded agents each address parts of the problem.

AI need not be the first discovery of human limitation to matter. It allows us to construct systems using different representations, alter their interfaces and computational resources, and observe where the same tasks succeed or fail. Questions once organized primarily around human self-experience gain additional objects of comparison.

None of this establishes that truth does not exist or that every perspective is equal. A route really may be impassable; a derivation may contain a contradiction; a model may fail under identifiable conditions. Finite knowers can acquire true and important knowledge. What they should relinquish is the habit of issuing a certificate of global completeness on the strength of local success.

Mature understanding can take a more concrete form: knowing which distinctions cannot be omitted, applying retained relations to new questions, identifying the conditions behind a conclusion, and locating a path back when those conditions fail.

We need not fit the world inside ourselves before we can begin to understand it. A practical and demanding requirement is to **keep our understanding open to correction by the world**.
